III. — 1444 / 1449

Soest Feud

Causes and course

Causes

  • Soest — wealthy Westphalian Hanseatic town under the Archbishopric of Cologne, but with extensive self-government
  • Archbishop Dietrich II von Moers pushes for tighter territorial control
  • Soest seeks support from Duke John I of Cleves — the Moers's main rival on the Lower Rhine
  • Dual character of the conflict: territorial lord vs. town, and at the same time Moers vs. Cleves-Mark
  • 5 June 1444 acceptance of Cleves, 25 June 1444 Fehdebrief

Course

  • 1444–1446: small-scale war — raids, plundering, skirmishes over castles (Bilstein, Fredeburg)
  • Failed Cologne attack on Duisburg (1445)
  • Turning point 1447: the Naumburg agreement redirects Czech mercenaries in the service of William III of Saxony into Westphalia
  • Summer campaign of 1447 ravages the borderlands; Soest itself cannot be taken
  • 1449 settlement — Soest retains its self-government under Cleves

The Czech mercenaries' route to Westphalia

Saxon Fratricidal War Sächsischer Bruderkrieg · 1446–1451

  • Brothers of the Wettin house: Elector Frederick II (the Gentle) × younger Duke William III in Thuringia
  • The Halle Partition Decree of 1445 was meant to settle the inheritance dispute — but remained ambiguous (the position of heir Ernest, the Ore Mountains silver, oversight of Erfurt)
  • Frederick insists on the indivisibility of electoral powers; William demands full sovereignty over his share; 1446 mobilization, William bets on recruiting foreign troops in Bohemia
  • Opl of Fictum (Apel Vitzthum the Elder of Roßla, ~1400–~1470) — William's chamberlain and the chief architect of Czech recruitment; contemporary sources accuse him of stirring William up against his elder brother
  • In January 1447 he launches a large-scale recruitment in Bohemia (Bedřich of Donín, Petr Holický ze Šternberka)

From Thuringia to Westphalia — Naumburg 1447

  • At this point Georg Spiegel, envoy of the Cologne archbishop Dietrich II von Moers, is operating in Saxony — his task is to use the Saxon Elector Frederick to recruit Czechs for the Soest Feud
  • 12 May 1447 · Naumburg · armistice between the brothers
  • Naumburg · Spiegel meets William together with the Czech field force — a three-party plan emerges:
    • Archbishop Dietrich takes over the Czech contingent as a whole
    • William III remains supreme commander (Soldunternehmer) and will lead the campaign into Westphalia
    • Elector Frederick helps indirectly and rids his Thuringia of William's army
  • Spiegel and Apel Vitzthum (Opl of Fictum) ride to Cologne to negotiate the contract terms

The 1447 Westphalian campaign — key points

Route and dates

  1. late May 1447 · Weimar — assembly of the six-thousand-strong army
  2. 30 May / 1 June · Frankenhausen — crossing of the Unstrut
  3. 2 June · Körner — proclamation of the military ordinance (last point in William's duchy)
  4. 14 June · Blomberg — taken and burned, followed by the plundering of the Lippe region (Hörn, Lemgo, Detmold, Herford)
  5. mid-June · Bielefeld — crossing of the Teutoburg Forest
  6. 20–29 June · Lippstadt — siege, unsuccessful
  7. 30 June · Soest — siege begun; the convent of St Walburga in the foreground occupied → artillery fire on the walls; a Soest cavalry sally is repulsed
  8. 19 July · general assault by the entire army on the walls
  9. 21 July — withdrawal from Soest, collapse of the coalition
  10. first ten days of August · return to Cheb via Mühlhausen

Campaign route

IV. — 1459 / 1463

Bavarian War

Bayerischer Krieg · Fürstenkrieg

Bavarian War — causes and main figures

Causes

  • Wittelsbach league system — a league of Landfrieden which started as a security alliance and gradually grew into a power bloc of southern Germany:
    • 17 Dec 1451 · founders: Louis IX the Rich (Bavaria-Landshut), Frederick I the Victorious (Palatinate) and Albert III (Bavaria-Munich) — all Wittelsbachs
    • gradually joined by Swabian Imperial Cities
    • 1455 · Duke Sigismund of Tyrol (a Habsburg, but an ally)
    • 1457 · Bohemian king Ladislaus the Posthumous
    • 1458 · Louis + Frederick renew the league with a lifelong alliance
  • Albrecht's ambition: to revive the duchy of Franconia under Hohenzollern rule → which brings him into conflict both with the Palatinate (Frederick I of the Palatinate) and with Bavaria (Louis IX of Bavaria)
  • Anti-Palatine alliance 1458: Albrecht + William III of Saxony + Baden + Württemberg + the Archbishop of Mainz

Main figures

Wittelsbachs and their allies

  • Louis IX the Rich — Bavaria-Landshut, mainstay of the league
  • Frederick I the Victorious — Elector Palatine
  • Albert III — Bavaria-Munich († 1460)
  • Albert VI of Austria — Habsburg, the emperor's brother
  • Sigismund of Tyrol — Habsburg, in the league from 1455

Anti-Palatine alliance (Hohenzollern + allies)

  • Albrecht III Achilles — Margrave of Brandenburg (Hohenzollern), main opponent
  • William III of Saxony — executor of the imperial ban, already familiar from Soest
  • Charles I of Baden — margrave
  • Ulrich V of Württemberg-Stuttgart — count
  • Diether von Isenburg — Archbishop of Mainz
  • Johann von Eich — Bishop of Eichstätt

Outside the open conflict

  • Emperor Frederick III of Habsburg — proclaims the imperial ban, stays clear of the action
  • Ladislaus the Posthumous († 1457) — joined the league in 1457
  • Jiří of Poděbrady — Bohemian king, from 1460 turns toward the Wittelsbachs
  • Pope Pius II — intervenes with Jiří against Czech recruitment by Louis

V. — 1468

Waldshut War

Waldshuterkrieg · Schaffhauser Krieg

Czech army arriving in 1468 to relieve besieged Waldshut · Amtliche Berner Chronik (1478–1483)

Waldshut War — causes and main figures

Causes

  • Second half of the 15th c.: escalating disputes between the Habsburg nobility (robber knights) and the towns in the South German region
  • North of the Rhine, the towns Schaffhausen (1454), Rottweil (1463) and Mülhausen (1466) turn to the Eidgenossen (Confederates) and conclude defensive alliances with them to fend off raids by the nobility

Two cores of the dispute

  • 1467 — Schaffhausen: Bilgeri von Heudorf tries to bring Schaffhausen back under Habsburg rule — at Anselfingen he captures the Schaffhausen mayor Hans Amstad, releases him only after a ransom of 1,800 guilders, whereupon the Confederates send troops
  • April 1468 — Mülhausen: backed by an alliance treaty with Bern and Solothurn, after years of undeclared small-scale war from the surrounding nobility, it goes on the offensive and attacks the noble villages of Rixheim and Sausheim. On 15 May 1468 Further Austria responds with a campaign of 4,000 men before Mülhausen and ravages the surroundings

Declaration of war

  • 18 June 1468 · Bern, Solothurn and Fribourg declare war on Duke Sigismund, and other Confederate towns soon follow

Main figures

Habsburgs and their side

  • Duke Sigismund the Rich — Tyrol and Further Austria
  • Werner von Schienen — commander of the defence of Waldshut (~800 men)
  • Bilgeri von Heudorf — instigator of the capture of Hans Amstad
  • Jakob Trapp — ducal court marshal, supply attempts
  • Thüring III von Hallwyl — Landvogt, fortifications at Albbruck
  • Louis IX of Bavaria — ally, sends ~1,600 Czech mercenaries

Confederates

  • Eberhard Ottikon (Zurich) — first captain
  • Hans Waldmann (Zurich) — later mayor and military leader
  • Felix Keller (Zurich) — captain of the Schaffhausen campaign
  • Petermann von Wabern, Niklaus von Scharnachthal, Niklaus von Diesbach (Bern)
  • Petermann Etterlin (Lucerne) — later chronicler

Mediators and external actors

  • Peter Rot — mayor of Basel, leader of the peace effort
  • Johann V von Venningen (Basel) and Hermann III von Breitenlandenberg (Constance) — bishops, mediators
  • Charles the Bold of Burgundy — later lends Sigismund 50,000 fl.
  • Diebold Schilling — Bern chronicler, the main source on the Czechs

The Waldshut Directive and its aftermath

  • 27 August 1468 · Waldshuter Richtung — Sigismund undertakes to pay 10,000 guilders in war contributions by 24 June 1469, and until then Waldshut and the Further Austrian part of the Black Forest serve as security
  • 23 June 1469 · the deadline is met and the contribution paid; the Schaffhausen mayor Hans Amstad, however, only gets his 1,800 fl. ransom back in 1476
  • November 1468 · by the treaty of Saint-Omer Sigismund borrows 50,000 guilders from Charles the Bold against the pledge of Breisgau and Upper Alsace, with Peter von Hagenbach becoming Landvogt of the pledge
  • 26 May 1469 · Emperor Frederick III (Sigismund's cousin) declares the Peace of Waldshut void and on 31 August proclaims an imperial ban over the Confederation — without real consequences
  • Territorial impact minimal — the only Confederate gain: the lordship of Wessenberg (Hottwil, Mandach) goes to Bern
  • 1474 · the Ewige Richtung under the patronage of Louis XI of France seals a permanent peace between the Habsburgs and the Confederation. After the fall of Charles the Bold at Nancy (1477) Sigismund takes the pledges back, without returning the 50,000 fl.

Czech mercenaries at Waldshut

"It happened that, while the camp lay before Waldshut, about sixteen hundred Bohemians were sent from the Empire by the duke to help the lords; and when they came, they nevertheless did not act in a warlike fashion against the Confederation, for they did more harm to friends than to the Confederation. Even so, the imperial duke, through his envoys, had it explained to the Confederation that he was bound by an agreement with Duke Sigismund of Austria under which he had to send him men, and that even if those whom he had sent were to remain somewhere on the way and not return home, this did not greatly concern him, for he was obliged to send them in honour and on the basis of the earlier written agreement they had made before this war; whatever else, however, he could do and arrange for peace and for the good in this matter, that he wished to do with goodwill, just as he and his predecessors had done in the earlier fifteen-year peaces and in other affairs." Diebold Schilling, Berner Chronik
  • Louis IX of Bavaria sent about 1,600 Czech mercenaries in order to honour his alliance treaty with Sigismund of Tyrol
  • He himself, however, did not want this conflict and on the contrary engaged in the mediation of peace talks
  • The Czechs therefore stood in the area of St. Blasien in the Black Forest and did not actually take part in the fighting
  • Illuminations of Schilling's chronicles depict the Czechs as "heathens and Orientals" with long hair — an allusion to their reputation as heretics
VI. — 1503 / 1505

War of the Landshut Succession

War of the Landshut Succession — causes and main figures

Causes

Dynastic background

  • George the Rich (Bavaria-Landshut), the last male representative of the Landshut line of the Wittelsbachs, had no male heir — only a daughter Elisabeth, who in 1499 married her cousin Rupert of the Palatinate
  • Elisabeth was at the same time niece of the Bohemian king Vladislaus II Jagiellon

Disputed will

  • 1496 · George wills the duchy to his daughter and Rupert — thereby breaking imperial law (male line) as well as the Wittelsbach inheritance treaties of 1392 and 1450, under which, on the extinction of the male line, the Landshut estate was to fall to the Bavaria-Munich branch
  • 1 December 1503 · George dies at Ingolstadt, and the will is immediately rejected by Albert IV the Wise and Maximilian I

Declaration of war

  • At the start of 1504 Rupert refuses Maximilian's compromises and his captains seize Landshut and Burghausen
  • 17 April 1504 · war formally breaks out, Maximilian declares an imperial ban on Rupert and Elisabeth and on 23 April grants the Landshut inheritance as a fief to Albert IV

Main figures

Palatine side

  • Rupert of the Palatinate (1481 – 20 Aug 1504) — heir of the Landshut estate, dies in the middle of the war
  • Philip of the Palatinate — Elector, Rupert's father
  • Georg von Wispeck — supreme commander at Schönberg

Bavaria-Munich and imperial side

  • Albert IV the Wise — Duke of Bavaria-Munich, claimant to the inheritance
  • Maximilian I — King of the Romans
  • Georg von Frundsberg — debuts at Schönberg with a Landsknecht regiment

Imperial-Bavarian side · ~150 horsemen

The attempt by Albert IV to recruit 6,000 Czech foot (negotiations on 4–5 February 1504 in Sušice, Klatovy and Domažlice) foundered for lack of funds. On the Czech side the negotiators were Chancellor von Smichau, Jan Klenovský of Klenová, Otík Kamýcký of Stropčice, Heřman Janovský of Janovice (later joined the Palatinate) and Lukavský. Václav Vlček of Čenov was also to have been won over — "the best Bohemian captain of his time" (though he no longer rode a horse and was carried about on a wagon).

Documented combatants were only minor individuals in Maximilian's army and ~150 Czech horsemen in the Nuremberg contingent (Erasbach near Berching, 8–10 September 1504).

Battle of Schönberg

Date
12 September 1504
Place
Hafenreuth field near Wenzenbach, north-east of Regensburg
Sides
Czech mercenaries in Palatine service vs. imperial-Bavarian coalition (Bavaria-Munich, Nuremberg, Brandenburg, Brunswick, Swabian League)
Commanders
Georg von Wispeck, Matyáš Libák of Radovesice (field captain of the infantry) vs. Maximilian I in person, Albert IV the Wise, Frederick of Brandenburg + sons Casimir and George, Eric of Brunswick, Andreas von Sonnenberg, Georg von Frundsberg (debut with the Landsknechts)
Forces
Czechs: ~3,000 foot, 180 wagons
Allies: ~4,000 foot, 1,200 – 1,600 horse, at least 8 – 12 guns
Casualties
Czechs: ~1,600 fallen, ~650 captured, ~180 wagons taken, 7 Nuremberg + 4 Strasbourg banners
Allies: ~200 fallen, of whom 40 knights; ~100 horses also killed
Duration
3 – 4 hours
Outcome
Crushing victory of the imperial-Bavarian coalition

Course of the battle

  • Sonnenberg's cavalry catches the Czech army retreating in the direction of Cham, and from 8 in the morning there are minor skirmishes with the retreating Czechs (~1.5 hours), in which Peter von Wintern, Maximilian I's cup-bearer, is killed
  • Maximilian fears that the Czechs will flee and sends a "forlorn hope" (verloren houif) against them — 400 horse under Margrave Casimir of Brandenburg; the attack forces the Czechs to deploy definitively into a defensive formation, and the brother of the chief field-treasurer falls in the action
  • Libák hastily arrays ~2,200 men for the defence on the Hafenreuth field between two small woods at Wenzenbach
  • The remaining 800 men he has build a Wagenburg behind their backs, on the neighbouring rise of Hühnerbuckl
  • Meanwhile Maximilian's main forces gradually arrive (after an exhausting night march) — among others Frundsberg's Landsknecht regiment and additional guns
  • By the constant pressure of all three components (cavalry, Landsknechts and artillery) the Czechs are ground down — the battle ends around 2 p.m. In the course of the fighting King Maximilian falls from his saddle; Duke Eric of Brunswick, himself wounded, helps him back up

  • Jahrsold (= Dienstgeld) — regular annual retainer for the military entrepreneur to keep his retinue ready (Ronšperk 200–500 fl./year)
  • Rüstgeld — one-off sum paid on conclusion of the contract, formally for arming the men (4 fl./foot + 8 fl./horse); the entrepreneur received it as a lump sum and distributed it himself
  • Kostgeld — flat allowance for the upkeep of the commander's personal retinue (squires, wagoners, fodder for the horses), outside the mercenary company
